\section*{Question 1}

\subsection*{(a)}

We have complete markets iff we can find a unique matrix $\Omega$ s.t.\ $X\Omega = I$, where
\[
X = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 10 & 0 \\ 20 & 10 & 10 \\ 30 & 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} | & | & | \\ w_1 & w_2 & w_3 \\ | & | & | \end{bmatrix},
\]
and each $w_i$ is a portfolio weight vector using A, B and C. For example, $(10, 20, 30)'$ is asset A's payoff across the three states.

$X\Omega = I$ means
\[
X w_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
X w_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
X w_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.
\]

Because $X$ is $3 \times 3$, $X\Omega = I$ has a unique solution iff $X$ is invertible, and
\[
X \text{ invertible} \iff \det(X) \neq 0 \iff \operatorname{rank}(X) = 3.
\]
\[
X = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 10 & 0 \\ 20 & 10 & 10 \\ 30 & 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}
= 10 \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Row reduction:
\begin{align*}
\text{row 3} - \text{row 2}: &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\text{exchange rows 1 and 2}: &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\text{row 2} - \text{row 1}: &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\text{row 3} - 3\cdot\text{row 1} - \text{row 2}: &\quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}
Thus $\operatorname{rank}(X) = 3$ and $X$ is invertible. $\Omega = X^{-1}$ exists and is unique, with $X\Omega = XX^{-1} = I$. Thus, the market is complete.

\subsection*{(b)}

Let $X$ be the matrix of $N = 3$ non-redundant asset payoffs, let $P$ be the price vector of the original assets, and let $P_c = (q_1, q_2, q_3)'$ be the price vector of the contingent claims. Use the LOP to solve for $P_c$ as a function of $X$ and $P$:
\[
P = \begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
X = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 10 & 0 \\ 20 & 10 & 10 \\ 30 & 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The LOP says that two assets with identical payoffs in every state of nature must have the same price. Therefore,
\[
X' P_c = P \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 20 & 30 \\ 10 & 10 & 10 \\ 0 & 10 & 10 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \\ q_3 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 8 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Dividing everything by 10:
\[
\begin{cases}
q_1 + 2q_2 + 3q_3 = 1.6 \\
q_1 + q_2 + q_3 = 0.8 \\
q_2 + q_3 = 0.6
\end{cases}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{cases}
q_1 = 0.2 \\
q_2 = 0.4 \\
q_3 = 0.2
\end{cases}
\]
Thus, $P_c = (0.2, 0.4, 0.2)'$.

\subsection*{(c)}

\[
P_D = \text{Payoff}(D) \cdot P_c
= \begin{pmatrix} 30 & 20 & 10 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix}
= 6 + 8 + 2 = 16.
\]

\subsection*{(d)}

\[
P(X) = E[MX] = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi(s) M(s) X(s).
\]
Since $q_s = \pi(s) M(s)$, we have $M(s) = q_s / \pi(s)$. With
\[
P_c = \begin{pmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\pi(s) = \begin{pmatrix} 1/4 \\ 1/2 \\ 1/4 \end{pmatrix}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
M(s) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.8 \\ 0.8 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
By definition, no arbitrage means that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An asset with payoff $\geq 0$ must have price $\geq 0$.
  \item An asset with payoff $\geq 0$ and a chance for payoff $> 0$ must have price $> 0$.
\end{enumerate}
We have $X \geq 0$, $\Pr(X > 0) > 0$, and $P(X) = E[MX] > 0$ (since $M > 0$). Thus, there is no arbitrage.

Moreover, $P(X) = E[MX] = 0.8\, E[X]$. The SDF stays constant across the three states, which implies it is risk-neutral.

\subsection*{(e)}

\begin{align*}
P_D = E[M X_D] &= \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi(s) M(s) X_D(s) \\
&= \tfrac14 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 30 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 20 + \tfrac14 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 10 \\
&= 6 + 8 + 2 = 16.
\end{align*}
This is the same as in Q1(c).

\subsection*{(a)}
I construct the unconditional linear SDF as

\[
\hat M_t^*=\hat a-\hat b'xR_t,
\]

where \(\hat b=\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\mu\) and \(\hat a=1+\hat b'\hat\mu\), imposing the normalization \(E[M^*]=1\).

Table \ref{tab:q2a-bm-sdf-parameters} reports the estimated SDF coefficients. The estimated SDF satisfies the unconditional pricing conditions, with $\widehat E[\hat M^*]=1$ and $\widehat E[\hat M^*xR_j]\approx 0$ for all 10 portfolios.

Figure \ref{fig:q2a-bm-sdf-coefficients} shows no clear monotonic relationship between the estimated \(b_j\) coefficients and book-to-market deciles. Instead, the coefficients fluctuate considerably across portfolios, with both positive and negative values.

This pattern arises because the coefficients are determined by both the expected excess returns and the covariance structure of the portfolios, as reflected in \(\hat b=\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat\mu\). Therefore, portfolios with higher average excess returns do not necessarily receive larger positive coefficients.

\subsection*{(c)}
Following the same approach as in parts (a) and (b), I construct the unconditional linear SDF using the combined 20 BM and MOM portfolios. Table \ref{tab:q2c-combined-sdf-parameters} reports the estimated SDF coefficients.

Figure \ref{fig:q2c-combined-sdf-coefficients} shows that the estimated coefficients vary considerably across both BM and MOM deciles, with positive and negative values and no clear monotonic pattern.
Compared with parts (a) and (b), the coefficients  change when the two sets of portfolios are combined, reflecting the additional covariance relationships and diversification opportunities.

Table \ref{tab:q2c-monthly-sharpe-ratios} reports monthly Sharpe ratios of 0.1919, 0.2581, and 0.3045 for the BM, MOM, and combined maximum Sharpe ratio portfolios, respectively. The combined portfolio achieves the highest Sharpe ratio, consistent with 
the theoretical prediction that expanding the investment opportunity set cannot reduce the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio.

This result is also consistent with the SDF volatility bound discussed in class. Under the normalization $E[M^*]=1$, the volatility of the constructed SDF equals the maximum Sharpe ratio. Thus, including both BM and MOM portfolios increases
the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio and the volatility of the corresponding SDF.

\subsection*{(d)}
Table \ref{tab:q2d-pricing-errors} reports the pricing
errors of the 20 BM and MOM portfolios under the three
SDFs constructed in parts (a), (b), and (c), using
\[
\hat{\alpha}_p
=\hat{E}[xR_p]
-\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(-\hat M^*,xR_p).
\]

The pricing errors are essentially zero for the
portfolios used to construct each SDF. Specifically,
the BM SDF prices all 10 BM portfolios, the MOM SDF
prices all 10 MOM portfolios, and the combined SDF
prices all 20 portfolios, with errors close to
numerical precision.

However, the BM and MOM SDFs exhibit nonzero pricing
errors when applied to portfolios outside their
respective construction sets. The maximum absolute
pricing error is 0.010157 for MOM portfolios under
the BM SDF, compared with 0.003450 for BM portfolios
under the MOM SDF.

These results suggest that an SDF constructed from
one set of portfolios does not necessarily price
another set of portfolios accurately. The combined
SDF eliminates the in-sample pricing errors by
incorporating both sets of portfolios. However,
this exact in-sample fit does not necessarily imply
good out-of-sample pricing performance.

\subsection*{(e)}
Following Question 2(c), I re-estimate the SDF using monthly excess returns of the 20 BM and MOM decile portfolios from July 1927 through December 1989:

\[
\hat M_t^*=\hat a-\hat{\mathbf b}'\mathbf{xR}_t.
\]

This gives the estimated intercept \(\hat a\) and coefficient vector \(\hat{\mathbf b}\).

I then hold the estimated coefficients fixed and construct the out-of-sample SDF using monthly excess returns from January 1990 through August 2026:

\[
\hat M_{OS,t}^*=\hat a-\hat{\mathbf b}'\mathbf{xR}_t.
\]

For each of the 20 portfolios, I calculate the out-of-sample pricing error as

\[
\hat\alpha_{OS,p}
=\widehat E[xR_p]
-\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(-\hat M_{OS}^*,xR_p),
\]

where both the expectation and covariance are estimated using only the out-of-sample observations. The resulting pricing errors are reported in Table \ref{tab:q2e-out-of-sample-pricing-errors}. All 20 portfolios exhibit positive out-of-sample pricing errors, suggesting that the estimated SDF does not fully capture their average excess returns.

% Transcribed from Q3a_Handwritten.pdf; intermediate expressions are preserved.
The investor solves
\[
\max_{\varpi_t} E_t[U(W_{t+1})]
\quad \text{s.t.}\quad
W_{t+1}=W_t\bigl(R_{f,t+1}+\varpi_t'xR_{t+1}\bigr).
\]
Assume that $W_t>0$, $W_{t+1}>0$ almost surely, and that the optimal
portfolio choice is interior. Under the usual regularity conditions, we
can derive the first-order condition by differentiating expected utility
w.r.t.\ $\varpi_t$.

This problem can be written as
\[
\max_{\varpi_t}
E_t\!\left[U\!\left(W_t\bigl(R_{f,t+1}+\varpi_t'xR_{t+1}\bigr)\right)\right].
\]
\[
\frac{\partial E_t\!\left[U\!\left(W_t\bigl(R_{f,t+1}+\varpi_t'xR_{t+1}\bigr)\right)\right]}
     {\partial\varpi_t}
=U'(W_{t+1})\cdot W_t\cdot xR_{t+1}.
\]
\[
U(w)=\frac{w^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma},
\qquad U'(w)=w^{-\gamma}.
\]
Thus, $U'(W_{t+1})=W_{t+1}^{-\gamma}$.
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial E_t\!\left[U\!\left(W_t\bigl(R_{f,t+1}+\varpi_t'xR_{t+1}\bigr)\right)\right]}
     {\partial\varpi_t}
&=E_t\!\left[U'(W_{t+1})\cdot W_t\right] \\
&=E_t\!\left[W_{t+1}^{-\gamma}\cdot W_t\cdot xR_{t+1}\right]=0.
\end{align*}
\[
E_t\!\left[\frac{W_t}{W_{t+1}^{\gamma}}xR_{t+1}\right]
=W_t E_t\!\left[\frac{1}{W_{t+1}^{\gamma}}xR_{t+1}\right]=0.
\]
Since $W_{t+1}^{\gamma}=(W_t\cdot R_{w,t+1})^{\gamma}$,
\[
E_t\!\left[\frac{1}{(W_t\cdot R_{w,t+1})^{\gamma}}xR_{t+1}\right]
=W_t^{-\gamma}E_t\!\left[R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}xR_{t+1}\right]=0.
\]
Since $W_t>0$, we have
\[
E_t\!\left[R_{w,t+1}^{-\gamma}xR_{t+1}\right]=0.
\]

\subsection*{(c)}
Using the identity weighting matrix, the GMM estimate of the relative risk aversion coefficient is $\hat\gamma=3.0064$, with a Newey–West standard error of 0.7207. The instrument denominator is the variance of gross equity returns over the 973 estimation observations from December 1940 through December 2021.

The estimated pricing errors for the equity portfolio and the managed portfolio are $-0.0038$ and 0.0019, respectively. Neither is statistically significant.

The joint $\chi^2(1)$ test yields a p-value of 0.4276, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis that both SDF pricing conditions hold simultaneously at conventional significance levels.
This suggests that the data do not provide sufficient evidence to reject the pricing restrictions implied by the power-utility SDF model.
